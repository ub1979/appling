The LastGlacier

For three hundred years the Varna ice crept down the valley. In one lifetime it went back up. This is the story of the people who measured it.

Words by Ida Morel · Drawings by the field team

01

The village that set its clocks by the ice

Every spring the children of Varnes ran to the edge of the ice to hear it crack. Their grandparents did the same, and theirs. The glacier was a calendar, a water tap and a wall against the north wind. Nobody thought of measuring it, because nobody thought it could leave.

That changed in 1962, when a schoolteacher hammered a painted stake into the gravel beside the snout and wrote the date on a card. Every year after, the class walked up to the stake and paced the distance to the ice.

“The first year we walked twelve steps. By the time I retired, we took the bus.”

The cards fill four shoe boxes now. Read in order, they are the slowest and saddest story the valley has ever told.

1962

The ice reaches the stake. The teacher's first card reads “12 steps, cold, Marta lost a mitten.”

1994

The snout has pulled back past the old sheep wall. A meltwater lake appears and the village names it, half joking, after the teacher.

2025

The ice is a pale tongue high in the cirque. The stake is a forty-minute climb from where the glacier now ends.
